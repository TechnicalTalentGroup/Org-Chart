Operations blueprint — building a $25M staffing firm

Prepared for CEO meeting · June 25, 2026

Operations mission

Create the systems, processes, reporting, and infrastructure that allow Sales and Recruiting to maximize spread while protecting margins and reducing operational risk. Operations captures revenue, protects revenue, reduces cost, scales systems, improves employee/contractor experience, reduces legal exposure, and increases efficiency. In the MIN framework: Sales generates opportunities, Recruiting delivers talent, Operations converts starts into profitable, scalable revenue.

How do we redesign Operations so leadership spends time building the company instead of processing transactions?

Six pillars of operations

1. Finance & revenue ops

Payroll (internal + contractor), invoicing, AR, collections, workers comp, risk management, profitability analysis, tax reporting, spread & P&L reporting, cash flow forecasting. Skills: payroll, accounting, pay/bill expertise, financial & risk analysis.

2. Contractor experience & back office

Onboarding, I-9/E-Verify, background/drug screens, contractor maintenance/QA, benefits admin, timekeeping support, retention, exit process, client onboarding requirements. Skills: HR, compliance, benefits, process management.

3. Internal HR & people ops

Internal onboarding, employment agreements, comp plans, reviews, PTO, handbook, engagement, culture. Skills: HR, employment law, leadership coaching.

4. Enablement & training

Training curriculum, recruiting/sales playbooks, SOPs, knowledge base, intranet. Skills: adult learning, staffing ops, documentation, coaching.

5. Marketing & brand ops

Website, SEO, social, case studies, sales collateral, salary guides, market intel, employer brand. Skills: copywriting, design, analytics, campaign strategy. Content production is currently handled by an existing VA; strategy and campaign ownership aren't yet assigned.

6. Systems, compliance & process improvement

Tech stack (ATS/CRM/payroll/timekeeping), safety/OSHA/EEO, insurance renewals, audits, workflow mapping, automation, vendor management, KPI dashboards. Skills: systems thinking, project management, vendor negotiation.

Proposed structure — a maturity curve, not 6 managers on day one

$25M is ~2.5x current revenue (~$10M projected by end of 2026) — a bigger runway than first framed. A 6-manager department is typical of $50M+ firms with much higher transaction volume per pillar — not the right ask for this growth step, and not the right ask for next year either. Pillars consolidate under fewer people until volume forces a split.

The capacity math Two people can't run 65% of their time on transactional volume that's still climbing, and find the bandwidth to build the automation that would bring that 65% down to a healthier 25%. Right now there's no one whose job is process improvement — it's 2% of completed work against a 25% target, because anyone who could do it is buried in entry work.

The risk angle With only two people, time-sensitive compliance work — I-9/E-Verify deadlines, workers comp/OSHA reporting, PSL approvals — has no backup. One person out sick or on PTO during a deadline window is a real exposure, not a hypothetical.

Vendor-managed work is still operations work

Owned & performed internally
  • Revenue/spread reporting, P&L oversight
  • Payroll processing
  • Workers comp, risk management, profitability analysis
  • Client & contractor onboarding
  • Process improvement, KPI reporting
Vendor-delivered, Ops-managed
  • Bookkeeping, reconciliations, tax filings
  • IT, security, device management, help desk
  • Benefits broker (open enrollment, compliance)

Reporting line: every vendor relationship reports to the Operations director for management, evaluation, and intervention — escalate to the CEO only when needed. Vendor management is itself operations headcount work, not a substitute for it.

Framing question for Glenn

"What should Operations own outright, and what should Operations own the management of (vendor relationships)?" — both still require Ops headcount and time. This turns the conversation from "do we need a person" to "what operating model and oversight structure does a $25M firm need," and sets up the people discussion (including the case for a third back-office hire) on top of an agreed structure rather than in place of one.
